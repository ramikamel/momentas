# Solo demo: momentas

Use an author browser and an incognito recipient browser. Label fictional samples and prepared outputs honestly.

## Three-minute narration

**0:00-0:20 — Problem:** "A parent can tell you where they grew up. A friend can tell you about a day that changed them. But the details they carry in their head are hard to share. momentas gives them something to show you, and something you can ask about."

**0:20-0:50 — Capture/review:** Show the bicycle sample and verbatim source quotes. "AI organizes what I said. I can correct it, add what mattered and approve what gets visualized."

**0:50-1:30 — Visualize/refine:** Show a real saved initial image and request "Make the parking lot feel enormous, and put Dad farther behind me." Show both versions. Cut long generation waits transparently. "This is how big the world felt to me."

**1:30-2:15 — Connect:** Publish. In the recipient window ask "Were you scared when you realized I'd let go?" Refresh the author view and type a personal reply. Refresh the recipient. "The answer comes from the person who lived the memory. The picture opens the conversation."

**2:15-2:40 — AI:** "Muse Spark extracts source-linked details. Muse Image renders the approved perspective and edits the selected image with a reference. The storyteller controls interpretation and publication."

**2:40-3:00 — Build/value:** "Built with Next.js, TypeScript, Tailwind, Supabase private storage and Meta APIs. Codex helped implement and review the app. Tests cover access, versioning, failed edits and the two-person exchange. Let me show you how I remember it."

## Rehearsal

1. Prepare a real generated image; do one live correction before judging to check latency.
2. Keep author and recipient browser contexts separate. Shared links cannot edit drafts.
3. Publish the chosen version deliberately. Draft edits stay private.
4. Wait for a question to appear in the conversation before refreshing the author. Wait for the author's reply to appear before refreshing the recipient.
5. Explain prepared footage and edits honestly; do not present a cached image as newly generated.
6. Check the deployed link and private image loading from another browser.

The presenter may play both fictional sample roles alone. Say so during judging. No voice transcription or synthesized narration was implemented.


## Extended solo demo: voice, a detail, and a second perspective

Use two browser contexts and clearly state that you are playing both fictional roles. Prepare two real Muse-generated perspectives in advance; identify them as saved results. Record your own voice for each role through the app if you want narration in the demo; do not use the transcription test fixture as a memory narration.

- 0:00–0:20: “We can describe what happened. What is harder to share is what stayed with each of us.”
- 0:20–0:45: Record a short account, show the reviewed transcript, and explain that the recorded voice is shared only by choice.
- 0:45–1:10: Show the prepared camera-like scene, press Add a detail, click a person or object, and write a brief note. Then open a saved pin: “The parking lot felt enormous to me.” The words are a selected, approved excerpt.
- 1:10–1:45: Open the second person's separate account and reveal both perspectives. The child remembers Dad far behind; Dad remembers watching the child ride away. Show one useful AI observation and its source quotations.
- 1:45–2:20: Ask a question on a pin. In the author context type a real sample reply, wait for it to save, then refresh the comparison. Emphasize that the reply comes from the person, not the model.
- 2:20–2:45: Explain Muse Voice transcription, Spark's structured extraction/comparison, Muse Image's photographic rendering, and immutable approved snapshots in Supabase.
- 2:45–3:00: “The picture starts the conversation. The other person's perspective is what changes it.”

Keep a labeled backup recording for network latency. Do not present a prepared image as newly generated, two independently generated scenes as exact visual identity matching, or a source-linked observation as historical proof.
